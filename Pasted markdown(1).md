## What the layer actually does

Cross-sectional momentum is relative momentum. It is the same idea as the RSI rotation from Bootcamp: you rank assets against each other and lean toward the ones showing the most relative strength. In Carver terms, every asset already carries a forecast built from the other layers, breakout, acceleration, EWMAC, skew, and so on. The cross-sectional layer takes those forecasts, compares them across the assets in your portfolio, and then adjusts the result for correlation through the FDM. That is it. Combine the layers, normalize price so everything sits on the same scale, and it becomes forecast versus the relative asset group.
The benefit and the limitation both live in one place: the data you feed it. The group you provide is the only yardstick it has. Whatever you put in front of it is what it compares to, and whatever you leave out, it cannot reach. If you do not feed it with intention, or you do not give it room to move somewhere genuinely different, that constraint is the algorithm.

## A plain-English version

Think of it like grading a class on a curve. You are not asking whether a student scored eighty percent in absolute terms. You are asking whether they beat the class average, and you back the ones who beat it by the most. Cross-sectional momentum does the same thing with assets: it does not care that an asset is up, it cares whether it is outperforming the rest of the group, and it leans into the leaders.
Now here is why the class matters. If your entire class is a set of identical twins who always score the same, grading on a curve is meaningless. Everyone sits on the average, nobody stands out, and picking the best tells you nothing. Worse, if they all fail the same exam on the same day, your whole bet fails at once. That is a portfolio of nothing but crypto: everything moves together, so the ranking has nothing to separate and the risk has nothing to offset. Give the model a real class instead, a mathematician, a historian, an athlete, a musician, and now when one stumbles another is often thriving. The ranking means something, and a bad day for one is not a bad day for all of them. Feeding this layer decorrelated assets is exactly that, giving it a class diverse enough that the curve, and the risk management built on top of it, can actually do their work.

## Single-asset Carver is legitimate

Before going further, it is worth saying that a Carver model on a single asset is completely fine. On its own the model is very good at managing risk, because it is comparing the asset only to itself through its own target price. Take Rocket Lab, a highly speculative, wildly volatile name that has sat at near liquidation-level drawdowns in its history. Run the Carver model on it and the drawdown comes in around 6.34 percent. Same story as Bitcoin in the earlier videos. Anything volatile, the model tends to keep on a leash. I am doing the same thing right now on Solana, where the model simply fits the asset well.
So single-asset Carver is not a compromise. It is a valid use, and it is also the honest fallback when your bucket is too correlated to support the cross-sectional layer at all. Plot the layer on QQQ, SPY, and BTC and the correlation is so high that removing the cross-sectional layer entirely often changes nothing. The layer is not executing, it has nothing to separate. At that point you may as well run the model on one asset and be done.

## The one rule: feed it decorrelated assets

The cross-sectional layer only earns its place when the assets in the bucket genuinely decorrelate. Hand it a pile of crypto and every asset produces a similar forecast, the correlation correction has nothing to correct toward, the weighted vote cannot distinguish anything, and you end up holding one concentrated bet wearing several tickers. The result is exactly the large drawdown the FDM is supposed to prevent.
Individual stocks are easier here, because Amazon and Pepsi genuinely move for different reasons, so a decorrelated pattern is easier to find on a basket of stocks than on a basket of coins. None of this is my invention. It is straight from Carver, who ran this across hundreds of futures instruments, and the requirement is the same at any scale: give it a healthy, diversified bucket or it cannot do its job.

## Long only versus long and short

A note on why my version is long only while Carver runs long and short. Mechanically there is nothing different about the short side. Carver rates forecasts across a range, and where his run from roughly plus twenty down to minus twenty, mine run from plus twenty down to zero. A negative forecast is simply a downside prediction that can act as a hedge, and coding the full range down to minus twenty is trivial. The reason I cap it at zero is the nature of the instruments.
Stocks, QQQ, and Bitcoin are stores of value. People hold them, the market generally drifts up, and there is no expiry, so you can hold into perpetuity. That behavior makes them correlate more and makes a long-only stance sensible. Futures, which is what Carver trades, are expiring contracts. You are not storing them, they can go to zero at expiry, and they let you short. Shorting is what unlocks the extra decorrelation: two opposite bets are, by definition, less correlated than two versions of the same bet, so the short side gives you both a hedge and a genuinely different pattern to hold. That is why Carver goes long and short, and why it makes sense for futures specifically. If you want to run this on futures, say for a prop firm like Top Step or FTMO that allows shorting, the mechanism is identical, you just code the range all the way down. ## Building a bucket that works

So how do you actually build a bucket that works? If you go the stock route, do it properly: use a real universe like the S&P 500, and reconstruct the constituents so you strip out survivorship bias, otherwise your backtest is quietly cheating. The other route is futures, where you may want to allow shorting as above.
Whatever the route, you are hunting for low correlation and, just as important, for movement. Carver needs assets that actually go somewhere. Something dead and sideways like Alibaba produces no forecast values at all, because there is nothing for the rules to catch. So you want assets that trend and that do not all trend together. Low beta helps. DBMF is a good example, a managed futures ETF with famously low, even negative, beta, exactly the kind of thing that decorrelates from an equity book. A small bucket can work too: gold and QQQ already do not correlate heavily and sit well together, and adding something like DBMF pushes the diversification further.
The other tool I keep coming back to is subsector ETFs. They are volatile, which Carver likes because it needs movement, and they are niche enough that full correlation between them is unlikely. Semiconductors through SMH, robotics and AI through BOTZ, quantum through QTUM, these are all slices of tech that the model handles well side by side. FRDM is a nice one, a freedom-weighted basket of emerging markets like Taiwan and Korea. BUZZ has a short history but works well specifically with cross-sectional momentum. As long as there is a real reason for the thing to rise, which these thematic funds usually have, it tends to do well, and because each one is fairly unique it is easier to find genuine decorrelation.
The trap is mixing scales. Drop QQQ into a basket of tech subsectors and you reintroduce noise and double-holding, because QQQ overlaps all of them. It will still make money, but it stops managing risk, because once the correlations collapse into sameness there is nothing left for the FDM to correct, and the drawdowns grow. One more standing rule: do not put stocks and crypto in the same bucket, and annualize correctly, 252 for stocks rather than 365, or the numbers lie to you.

## The code

The notebook is attached below as a download, and most of it is scaffolding from earlier videos, so I will not re-explain the pieces you have already seen. EWMAC, the forecast and breakout rules, the single EWMA base, the full stack, and the clipping are all defined as before. There is a synthetic-data fallback so the notebook still runs offline, say on a plane, and a fixed random seed so results replicate, which not everyone bothers with. The selection cell holds the subsector ETF basket, the uncorrelated, high-volatility mix described above.
The new work is the cross-sectional layer, and it is built from a few small functions.
The first, `relative_price`, turns a raw price series into a fair, comparable version. It strips out the effect of an asset's own volatility so you can see how much it has really moved, and puts every asset on an equal footing. Internally, `PN` builds the normalized price series over the chosen window, `A` is the simple average of those normalized prices across the group, and `R` subtracts that benchmark from the target's normalized price. When `R` is rising, the target is beating the group. It runs over two horizons, 14 and 80 days, kept deliberately short to keep the degrees of freedom low.
The cross-sectional signal function sits on top of that. For the chosen assets, here the crypto set, it checks whether each is beating the group average over those two horizons, and where there is outperformance it converts that into a trading signal. It then scales the signal to the right strength, blends the two windows, and clips the extremes so no single outlier runs away with it. The per-window pieces are held in `sub_CS`, and the combined result, `CS`, averages the two windows and applies a small boost. The plots that follow show the normalized price against the asset class, whether it is outperforming, and a sample of the resulting forecast values.
Then comes the correlation correction, the FDM. This function looks at how much the different rules tend to agree or disagree and collapses that into a single number, the FDM value, which tells you how much real benefit you actually got from blending them. Skip this correction and concentration rises and the system underperforms. On the futures side, shorting would widen that benefit further.
The blending function does the assembly. It takes any number of signals, weights them the way you have chosen, and applies the diversification boost while correcting for correlation. Where rules are similar it dampens their combined strength so noise does not masquerade as signal. In the code, `names` collects the rule names, `F` stacks all the forecasts side by side into a table, `w` pulls and normalizes the weights, `C` builds the correlation matrix and cleans up any missing values, and `FDM` calls the correlation step, applies the correction, multiplies the rules by their weights, sums them, applies the boost, and clips the result back into range.
Finally, `full_carver` ties everything together. It builds the volatility stack for the chosen asset, combines all the layers, blends them with the diversification boost from the FDM, and applies volatility attenuation that eases off when the market gets shocking, which I covered in an earlier video. It returns the forecasts and weights, and from there it is straight into the backtest.
